The Beginner's Starter Kit: What You Actually Need (And What You Don't)

FROM THE ACADEMY

Every beginner does the same thing when they get serious about detailing: they open a forum, a YouTube comment section, or a Facebook group, and they start buying. A soap here, a coating there, three different microfiber towel brands because someone swore one was better. Within a month they've got forty bottles on a shelf and no real sense of what any of them are actually for.

That's not a starter kit. That's a distraction with a barcode.

Why more products makes you worse, not better

When you own forty products, you spend more mental energy deciding what to use than actually using it well. You never get repetitions with any single product, so you never build real feel for how it behaves — how much pressure it needs, how long it dwells, what it looks like when it's working versus when it's not. Skill comes from repetition. Repetition requires narrowing down, not stacking up.

The fastest way to get good is to get boring: pick a small kit and use it on every job until it's second nature.

That's it. That's the list. Everything else is something you add later, once you've actually mastered what's already in your hands — not because a forum told you to buy it.

When you're ready to start polishing

Once the basics are second nature and you want to start learning paint correction with a dual-action polisher, don't grab the most aggressive compound you can find. Start with something forgiving that teaches you technique before speed. I recommend 3D ONE as a first polish for anyone getting into DA polishing — it's an all-in-one that gives you room to learn without punishing small mistakes the way a heavier cut compound will.

The real cost of the forty-bottle shelf

It's not just the money, though that adds up fast. It's the time. Every hour spent comparing products online is an hour not spent on a car, not building the actual skill that pays your bills. Beginners who narrow down early get good faster — not because they're more talented, but because they're not splitting their attention forty ways.

Start small. Get good at small. Expand once small stops being a challenge.
